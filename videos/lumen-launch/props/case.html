Kettle and Co.: Brewing Growth in Ten Minutes

A fictional teaching case prepared for class discussion.

On a humid Monday morning in Bengaluru, Ananya Rao, co-founder and chief executive of Kettle and Co., looked at the dashboard on her laptop and did not like what she saw. Orders through quick commerce apps had tripled in a year. Margins on those same orders had fallen by almost half.

Kettle had started in 2019 as a single tea counter outside a tech park. By 2026 it ran 140 stores across six cities, sold packaged chai premixes in supermarkets, and had become one of the most searched beverage brands on Blinkit, Zepto and Swiggy Instamart. The board wanted a plan by the end of the quarter: lean further into ten-minute delivery, or pull back and protect the stores.

The Quick Commerce Bet

Quick commerce had changed how urban India shopped. Dark stores within two kilometres of most homes promised delivery in ten minutes. For Kettle, the platforms offered reach that no store expansion could match. A premix listed on three apps was visible in more than nine hundred dark stores overnight.

The reach came at a price. Platforms charged commissions between 18 and 25 percent, asked brands to fund discounts during sale weeks, and increasingly sold their own private label tea next to Kettle's. "We built their tea aisle for them," Rao told her team. "Now they are learning what sells and making it themselves."

The Stores

The stores told a different story. Average order value in a Kettle store was 240 rupees, against 410 rupees online, but store customers came back an average of nine times a month. Stores also carried the brand: most first-time buyers on the apps said they had tried Kettle in a store first.

Opening a new store cost about 38 lakh rupees and took fourteen months to break even. Rao's chief financial officer, Vikram Sethi, argued that every rupee spent on new stores was a rupee not spent on the channel that was actually growing.

Three Options

The leadership team had narrowed the choice to three paths. The first was to go all in on quick commerce: shift marketing spend to the apps, launch app-exclusive flavours, and slow store openings to ten a year. The second was a direct channel: build Kettle's own ten-minute delivery from its stores, which already sat in dense neighbourhoods, and use the apps only for premixes. The third was to stay balanced, holding store growth at thirty a year while negotiating harder with the platforms.

Each path had its champion. The head of growth wanted the first. The head of operations believed the stores could double as delivery hubs. Sethi worried that the third option was simply a way to avoid deciding.

The Decision

Rao closed the dashboard. Whatever she chose, she would need to explain to the board why the fastest-growing channel was also the least profitable one, and what Kettle would look like in five years if the platforms became its largest competitor.
